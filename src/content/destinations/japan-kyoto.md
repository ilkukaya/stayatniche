Japan rewards travel at any level of engagement. As a first-time visitor absorbing the surface, the temples, the bullet trains, the impeccable food, it is consistently ranked among the world's most satisfying travel experiences. As a repeat visitor going deeper, learning the vocabulary of a tea ceremony, walking a mountain pilgrimage trail between ancient lodges, spending a week in a single ryokan learning the rhythms of its seasonal cuisine, it reveals a depth of cultural and aesthetic refinement that few other countries approach.


Kyoto served as Japan's imperial capital for over a thousand years (794 to 1869) and remains the country's cultural centre of gravity. The city's 17 UNESCO World Heritage Sites include Zen gardens of mathematical precision, gilded temple pavilions, and the hillside shrine complex of Fushimi Inari with its famous serpentine avenue of 10,000 torii gates. But Kyoto's finest experiences resist simple categorisation: a morning watching monks at dawn prayer in Nanzen-ji's garden courtyard; a kaiseki dinner of 12 courses in a machiya (wooden townhouse) where each dish references the season's exact moment; an apprentice geiko glimpsed at dusk on the stone-flagged lanes of Gion's Hanamikoji Street.


Japan's ryokan, the traditional inn, typically family-run, built around the same site for generations, is one of the world's great accommodation formats. The finest ryokan combine tatami-floored rooms, futon bedding, private or shared outdoor onsen (geothermal hot spring baths), and kaiseki cuisine into an experience that functions as a total immersion in Japanese aesthetic values: wabi-sabi (the beauty of imperfection), ichigo ichie (the unrepeatable nature of each moment), and omotenashi (a form of hospitality that anticipates needs before they are expressed).

The onsen ryokan tradition is most richly experienced away from Kyoto itself, in mountain resort towns like Kinosaki Onsen (where seven public bathhouses are strolled between in yukata robes on the willow-lined canal streets), Kurokawa Onsen (a village of 30 ryokan with open-air rotenburo baths above a river gorge), or Hakone (with its views of Fuji across the caldera lake). These are the closest Japanese equivalent to the concept explored by StayAtNiche's [bubble hotels](/categories/bubble-hotels) and [cave hotels](/categories/cave-hotels), intimate, hands-on experiences defined by their physical environment.


The Kii Peninsula, south of Osaka, contains the ancient pilgrimage routes of Kumano Kodo, mountain trails through cryptomeria forest connecting a series of grand shrines. The multi-day walk (4–6 days from Kii-Tanabe to the Kumano grand shrines) is the closest Japan comes to the Camino de Santiago in terms of both difficulty and spiritual atmosphere. Simple mountain guesthouses (minshuku) along the route provide meals, futon beds, and the conversation of fellow pilgrims.

Further afield, the mountain village of Shirakawa-go (UNESCO World Heritage) in the Japanese Alps preserves a cluster of farmhouses with steeply pitched thatched roofs (gassho-zukuri, literally 'hands in prayer') in a landscape of rice paddy and mountain backdrop. Several operate as guesthouses, offering the chance to sleep in a structure built in the 17th century and still functioning as it was designed to.


Japan's Shinkansen (bullet train) network connects its major cities in journey times that rival or beat flying once airport transit time is factored in: Tokyo to Kyoto in 2h15m, Kyoto to Hiroshima in 1h25m. IC transport cards work smoothly across the country. Japan is more English-accessible than reputation suggests in tourist areas, but downloading Google Translate with Japanese offline is worthwhile for rural travel and restaurant menus.

The cost of travel in Japan has become substantially more accessible following the Yen's weakening against the Dollar and Euro since 2022, what was once considered an expensive destination now represents remarkable value for Western travellers, particularly at the level of ryokan accommodation and kaiseki cuisine.
