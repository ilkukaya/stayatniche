Kerala operates at a different frequency than the rest of India. There is no less color, the coconut palms are impossibly green, the backwater reflections are mirror-sharp, the spice markets smell of cardamom and black pepper in equal measure, but the pace is gentler, the roads are better, the literacy rate is the highest in the country, and the cuisine is extraordinary. Kerala rewards slowness more than almost any other destination in India.


The Alleppey backwaters are a 900-kilometer network of lagoons, lakes, rivers, and artificial canals cutting through the coastal lowlands between Kochi and Kollam. The kettuvallam, a traditional rice barge converted into a floating hotel, has become the defining symbol of Kerala travel, and with good reason. These handbuilt wooden boats, traditionally used to transport rice from the paddy fields of Kuttanad to the coast, now carry beds, bathrooms, and a cook serving freshly caught fish and coconut curries from a galley kitchen at the stern.

The experience is best from late afternoon into the following morning: mooring in a remote canal as the light fades, watching kingfishers and herons work the bank, eating dinner on the deck as the frogs begin, and waking to nothing but water and birdsong. The best-quality houseboats have air-conditioned bedrooms, sundeck seating, and cooks who produce proper Kerala meals rather than tourist approximations.


Kerala's inland flank rises quickly from the coastal plain into the Western Ghats: a mountain range of exceptional biodiversity, tea and coffee and cardamom plantations, and wildlife reserves protecting significant populations of elephants and tigers. The Wayanad and Munnar highlands are the main attraction: mornings in the tea gardens, evenings watching elephant herds cross a forest road, and nights in boutique plantation bungalows or [treehouse hotels](/categories/treehouse-hotels) built in the forest canopy above the coffee crop.

Periyar Wildlife Reserve at Thekkady offers boat safaris on the Periyar Reservoir, where elephants come to drink and bathe on the forested banks. Bamboo rafting through the reserve's core area, a guided experience limited to small groups, penetrates the forest in a way that conventional safari vehicles cannot.


The city of Kochi is Kerala's cultural anchor, specifically the Fort Kochi and Mattancherry peninsulas, where five centuries of Portuguese, Dutch, British, Jewish, and Chinese presence left behind a layered architectural and cultural palimpsest. The Chinese fishing nets that dominate the harbor photographs are functional (fishermen still work them at high tide) and the lanes between the Paradesi Synagogue, the Mattancherry Palace, and the contemporary art galleries and restaurants of Fort Kochi are the most enjoyable urban walking in Kerala.

The Kochi-Muziris Biennale, held in Fort Kochi every two years, has cemented the city's reputation as India's most artistically significant port.
