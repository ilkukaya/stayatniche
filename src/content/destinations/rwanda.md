Rwanda's comeback story is one of the most remarkable in modern history. The country that experienced one of the 20th century's worst genocides in 1994 — 800,000 people killed in 100 days — has rebuilt itself into a nation that consistently tops African rankings for governance, safety, cleanliness, and economic growth. Kigali is the continent's most orderly capital. The roads are maintained. The plastic bag ban, in place since 2008, has made Rwanda's landscape visibly cleaner than virtually any other African country. And the Virunga volcanic mountains in the northwest harbour the world's last wild population of mountain gorillas.

## Mountain Gorillas — The Encounter

There are approximately 1,000 mountain gorillas left on earth — all of them in a single transboundary ecosystem spanning Rwanda, Uganda, and the DRC. Rwanda's Volcanoes National Park is home to roughly a dozen habituated gorilla families, each visited once per day by a group of eight trekkers maximum. The one-hour encounter that results — watching a family of 20 or 30 gorillas go about their morning routine at distances of three to ten metres, occasionally locking eyes with a silverback of profound physical authority — is consistently described by those who have done it as among the most affecting experiences of their lives.

The trek itself ranges from 30 minutes to a full day depending on where the assigned family has moved since the previous morning. Park rangers track each family daily, and scouts radio ahead to guide trekkers to the gorillas' location. The forest — bamboo at lower elevations, then Hagenia woodland, then moorland approaching the volcanic summits — is beautiful in its own right.

## Rwanda's Conservation Model

Rwanda has made a strategic decision to position gorilla trekking as a high-value, low-volume experience. The $1,500 permit price is not arbitrary — it functions as a conservation funding mechanism, with 10% of all park revenue distributed directly to communities living on the park boundary. The result is that local families have a direct economic stake in gorilla conservation: their livelihoods improve when gorillas thrive. Rwanda's gorilla population has grown from 620 in 2010 to over 1,000 today — proof that the model works.

The [safari lodges](/categories/safari-lodges) immediately surrounding Volcanoes National Park have responded to this high-value ethos by building some of Africa's most sophisticated wilderness properties. Stone-walled lodges with fireplaces (the altitude means evenings are genuinely cold at 2,300m), forest-edge locations where golden monkeys visit the gardens, and ranger-guided night walks round out experiences that extend well beyond the gorilla trek itself.

## Beyond the Volcanoes

Nyungwe Forest in the southwest is one of Africa's oldest and most biodiverse rainforests — home to chimpanzees (trackable on guided habituations), 13 primate species total, 300 bird species, and a canopy walkway suspended 60 metres above the forest floor that offers a genuinely vertiginous perspective on the forest ecosystem. Akagera National Park in the east has been restored to full Big Five status following the reintroduction of lions in 2015 and black rhino in 2017 — a conservation success story that mirrors Rwanda's wider national narrative.

Lake Kivu, on the western border with the DRC, is a vast freshwater lake surrounded by volcanic hills and dotted with islands that can be explored by boat. Its surface is calm and warm; its depths contain dissolved carbon dioxide and methane from volcanic activity below — an unusual geological circumstance that the Rwandan government is literally mining for energy. Beach lodges on Kivu's shore offer a relaxed counterpoint to the physical demands of gorilla trekking.

## Practical Considerations

Kigali International Airport receives direct flights from Brussels, London, Nairobi, Addis Ababa, and increasingly from further afield as Rwanda positions itself as a regional aviation hub. The drive from Kigali to Volcanoes National Park takes approximately two and a half hours on excellent paved roads — refreshingly straightforward by East African standards. Rwanda's domestic tourism infrastructure is among the best in continental Africa.
