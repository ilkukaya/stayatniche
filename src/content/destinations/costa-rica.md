Costa Rica likes to remind visitors that it abolished its military in 1948, redirecting defence spending toward education and healthcare. The country runs on 99% renewable energy. A quarter of its territory is protected national park or biological reserve. These are not marketing talking points, they are structural choices that have made Costa Rica the most environmentally successful small country on earth, and incidentally the most rewarding destination in the Americas for wildlife travel.


The numbers are staggering for a country smaller than West Virginia. Costa Rica hosts over 900 bird species, 200 mammal species, 35,000 insect species, and 1,200 orchid varieties. The reason is geography: the country sits at the junction of two tectonic plates and two major ocean currents, where North and South American species lists overlap. Cloud forests, tropical dry forests, rainforests, mangroves, and coral reefs exist within hours of each other.


The Osa Peninsula's Corcovado National Park is where Costa Rica's biodiversity concentrates to almost absurd density. Tapirs wade through rivers. Scarlet macaws fly in pairs over the canopy. Jaguars, rarely glimpsed but genuinely present, patrol the beach at night. Access requires permits and a registered guide, which is exactly the point: controlled access has kept Corcovado in a condition that other rainforests can only aspire to. The lodges on the Osa Peninsula, small, solar-powered, reached by small aircraft or boat, represent the model for what eco-accommodation should be.


Costa Rica invented the modern eco-lodge. The best properties here combine minimal footprint with maximum immersion: hanging bridge walkways through the canopy, night walks with naturalist guides, frog ponds and butterfly gardens that are research stations as much as hotel amenities. Staying in a treehouse lodge above a cloud forest at Monteverde, or a solar-powered outdoor cabin on the edge of Corcovado, is to experience travel that has genuinely worked out how to be both luxurious and responsible.


The Arenal Volcano, a near-perfect cone visible for miles, anchors a region of hot springs, hanging bridges, and waterfall hikes that combines adventure with relaxation. La Fortuna town services the volcano's tourism industry with everything from white-water rafting to canyoning. The thermal hot springs, fed by geothermal heat from the volcano, range from elaborate resort pools to natural river pools accessible with a local guide.
