Norway is one of the few places in Europe where the word "wilderness" still means something. The fjords, carved by glaciers over millions of years into slot canyons of sea water flanked by near-vertical cliff faces, stretch inland for up to 200 kilometres from the coast. The country's western edge is a fractal of inlets, peninsulas, and island chains that adds up to a total coastline, when measured in full, of over 100,000 kilometres.


Geirangerfjord and Nærøyfjord, both UNESCO World Heritage sites, represent the apotheosis of the Norwegian fjord landscape. Geiranger in particular, with its waterfalls tumbling from abandoned mountain farms, cruise ships dwarfed to toys by the cliff walls, is so cinematic it can feel unreal. The experience of sailing through on a smaller vessel, or kayaking in the silence of early morning, grounds it in physical reality.


The Lofoten Islands, above the Arctic Circle and well within the domain of the midnight sun and northern lights, are Norway's most dramatic and photogenic region. Fishing villages perched on stilts over sea, their red and yellow rorbu (fishermen's cabins) converted to tourist accommodation, offer the kind of stay that bears no resemblance to a standard hotel. The mountains rise directly from the water, the light in summer is horizontal and extraordinary, and the cod fishing heritage is still very much alive.


For those who want to push further, Svalbard sits at 78°N, accessible from Oslo by a 3-hour flight. Polar bears outnumber humans here. The glacier landscape, vast, white, and almost entirely uninhabited, is accessible by snowmobile, dog sled, or expedition ship. No visa is required for any nationality, making it one of the most accessible pieces of the High Arctic on earth.


Norway's defining characteristic is its relationship with light. The midnight sun of June and July, when the sun never fully sets and the world exists in a continuous golden hour, is memorable. The polar night of deep winter, when the sun doesn't rise for weeks, creates the darkness necessary for the clearest aurora displays in mainland Europe. Between these extremes, the country cycles through every quality of light imaginable.
