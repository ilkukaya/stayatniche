The Seychelles exists at the top of most travellers' mental list of dream destinations, an archipelago of granitic and coralline islands in the western Indian Ocean, far from major shipping lanes, that have cultivated a reputation for white sand, turquoise water, and extreme discretion. The reality is everything the fantasy promises and then some, particularly for travellers who extend beyond the well-known inner islands to explore the extraordinary outer atolls, among the most remote and clear marine environments remaining on earth.


The inner Seychelles, Mahé, Praslin, La Digue, and their satellites, are unusual among tropical island destinations for a fundamental geological reason: they are the world's only oceanic islands made of granite. Ancient continental rock, these islands rose from the Indian Ocean as Africa and India split apart; their age (approximately 750 million years) means they carry none of the surrounding coral islands' biological characteristics and everything that makes them visually distinct. The enormous rounded granite boulders that frame beaches like Anse Source d'Argent on La Digue are unique on earth: stacked, smoothed by millennia of wave action, and coloured in shades of rose, amber, and grey that shift dramatically with the light.


The Vallée de Mai on Praslin Island is a UNESCO World Heritage Site of extraordinary botanical significance. The palm forest here, primeval, dense, cathedral-lit, is home to the coco de mer palm, which produces the world's largest seed (up to 25 kilograms) in a shape that has made it an object of mythology and fascination for centuries. The forest also shelters the black parrot, endemic to Praslin and found nowhere else on earth. Walking through Vallée de Mai at dawn, with the canopy closing overhead and the light filtering through ancient fronds, is genuinely unlike any other forest experience in the tropics.


The Seychelles has developed the private island resort concept to an extraordinary degree. North Island, Frégate, Bird Island, Denis Island, and a dozen others offer total exclusivity: your party, or a small number of villas sharing an island, served by a staff who have learned your preferences within hours of arrival. The environmental programmes on these islands are often genuinely impressive: North Island, for example, has actively restored native vegetation and reintroduced species driven extinct by colonial-era predator introduction.

Overwater bungalows in the Seychelles are built over lagoons of extraordinary clarity, the water visibility in the outer atolls reaches 30 metres, and the marine life directly below a private deck can include reef sharks, rays, and sea turtles. Several of the inner island resorts now offer overwater villa categories that place guests directly above living reef systems within snorkelling distance of the beach.


Aldabra Atoll, accessible by liveaboard expedition vessel from Mahé, is one of the world's great wildlife destinations and among its least visited. The atoll's 155,000 Aldabra giant tortoises, the world's largest tortoise population, evolved in complete isolation and were saved from extinction by the refusal of various scientists, most notably Charles Darwin, to allow the island to be developed. Walking among giant tortoises on Aldabra, which has no permanent human population and receives only a handful of research vessels per year, is an experience of wildlife remoteness matched only by the Galápagos.


Mahé, the largest island and home to the capital Victoria, is the arrival hub and offers the most varied accommodation and dining. The Morne Blanc hiking trail in the national park climbs through cloud forest to a summit with views over the island and surrounding ocean. The National Botanic Gardens in Victoria display the full range of Seychellois endemic species in a compact and beautifully maintained setting. Inter-island flights to Praslin take 15 minutes; the ferry (La Digue from Praslin) takes 30 minutes. A week that combines Mahé, Praslin, and La Digue, with a day trip to Cousin Island for the seabirds, represents a remarkably complete introduction to the inner Seychelles.
