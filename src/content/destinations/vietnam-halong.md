Vietnam repays travel across its full length. The country's distinctive geography, a long, narrow strip of land squeezed between mountains and sea, means that each region has a distinct culture, climate, and culinary identity, shaped by the different kingdoms and empires that controlled it over 2,000 years of written history. Travelling Vietnam from north to south (or south to north) over three to four weeks is one of Southeast Asia's great itineraries, a journey through landscape as much as a journey through time.

## Hạ Long Bay: The Iconic Image

Hạ Long Bay's 1,969 karst limestone islands, rising sheer from the emerald waters of the Gulf of Tonkin, some hollow with cave systems, others with hidden lagoons accessible only at low tide, is one of the world's most recognised landscapes. The bay has been designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site twice (for scenic value and geological significance), and the nightly spectacle of a hundred junk boats anchored among the islands, their lights reflecting in calm water, has become one of Asia's defining images.

The quality of the experience varies dramatically with the vessel. Large cruise junks carrying 40 or more passengers spend most of their time alongside other large cruise junks, visiting the same mass-market caves on a conveyor-belt schedule. Boutique overnight cruises on smaller vessels, ideally carrying 10 to 20 passengers on traditionally designed wooden junks, can anchor in quieter corners of the bay, launch kayaks into unexplored lagoons, and provide a genuine sense of isolation among the limestone towers. The investment in a better boat is among the most directly rewarding upgrades available in Vietnam travel.

## The Central Heartland

Hội An Ancient Town, 30 kilometres south of Đà Nẵng, is one of Southeast Asia's most beautifully preserved historic cities. The merchant quarter, a compact area of 15th to 19th-century trading houses built in a fusion of Japanese, Chinese, and Vietnamese architectural styles, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site that continues to function as a living town rather than a preserved exhibit. The narrow lanes, lantern-lit at night, contain an extraordinary density of tailoring shops, ceramic studios, cooking schools, and restaurants.

Hội An's cuisine is considered one of Vietnam's regional finest: cao lầu (noodles with pork and greens eaten only with the specific water drawn from ancient wells in the old town), white rose dumplings, and bánh mì (the Vietnamese baguette sandwich, executed here to a standard that explains why it has become a global food phenomenon) are the essential orders.

Huế, 100 kilometres north of Đà Nẵng, was Vietnam's imperial capital under the Nguyen dynasty (1802–1945). The citadel, a walled complex modelled on Beijing's Forbidden City, contains the Forbidden Purple City of the emperors, now largely in ruins but undergoing restoration. The surrounding moat, the seven-colour ceramic towers, and the elaborate royal tombs scattered through the hills south of the city make Huế one of Vietnam's most historically rich destinations.

## Sapa and the North

The terraced rice paddies of the Muong Hoa Valley near Sapa in the far northwest, constructed over centuries by H'Mong, Dao, and Tay minority communities, are some of Southeast Asia's most spectacular agricultural landscapes. The terraces, best seen when flooded in April or during harvest in September, cascade down steep mountain slopes in precise curves that mirror the valley's contours. Homestays in minority villages in the Sapa region provide one of Vietnam's most genuine cultural encounters.

## Phong Nha: The World Beneath

Phong Nha-Kẻ Bàng National Park in central Vietnam contains the world's largest cave system, including Son Doong, discovered by a local farmer in 1991 and first fully explored by British cavers only in 2009. Son Doong's main passage is 5 kilometres long, 150 metres wide, and 200 metres tall, large enough to contain a New York city block. Access is strictly limited to a few hundred visitors per year on guided expeditions that involve camping inside the cave for four nights.

For those without the time or budget for Son Doong, the Paradise Cave (31km long, open to visitors for 1km), Phong Nha Cave (river cave accessible by boat), and Dark Cave (zipline entry, mud pool, and cave kayaking) offer extraordinary cave experiences at various price points. Several boutique [jungle lodges](/categories/jungle-lodges) near the national park entrance have positioned themselves as bases for multiple days of cave exploration.
