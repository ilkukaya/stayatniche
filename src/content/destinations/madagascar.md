Madagascar is the world's fourth largest island, and biologically speaking, it might as well be a different planet. When it broke away from the African continent approximately 165 million years ago and from India 88 million years ago, it carried with it an ancestral biological community that proceeded to evolve in complete isolation. The result is an island where 90% of all species found — mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and plants — exist nowhere else on earth. There are 107 species of lemur. There are 83 species of chameleon (half the world's total). There are plants that have evolved to resemble stones. Madagascar is not merely a wildlife destination; it is the most extraordinary example of evolutionary divergence accessible to travellers.

## Lemurs — The Ancient Primates

Lemurs are prosimian primates — an older evolutionary branch than monkeys and apes — that thrived in Madagascar's isolation after the island separated from Africa before the arrival of more competitive primate groups. Today's 107 species range from the mouse lemur (the world's smallest primate at 30 grams) to the indri (the size of a small child, with a haunting territorial call audible for 3 kilometres through the rainforest). Ring-tailed lemurs — the species most familiar from wildlife documentaries, sun-worshipping with arms outstretched in Isalo's sandstone canyon — are just the beginning.

The best lemur tracking experiences are in the eastern rainforest corridor: Andasibe-Mantadia National Park, within 3 hours of Antananarivo, for the indri; Ranomafana for the elusive golden bamboo lemur (discovered only in 1986); and the Kirindy Forest near Morondava for nocturnal encounters with mouse lemurs, sportive lemurs, and the giant jumping rat — one of Madagascar's most bizarre endemic mammals.

## The Avenue of the Baobabs

Twenty kilometres north of Morondava, a dirt track runs between a double row of ancient baobab trees — some over 800 years old, their bulbous trunks reaching 20 metres height, their branches reduced to sparse candelabra twigs above. In the golden light of an hour before sunset, this landscape is otherworldly — it looks like a dream of a forest rather than an actual one, and has become one of the most photographed landscapes in Africa despite being entirely inaccessible for several months of the year. The baobabs are remnants of a forest cleared for agriculture centuries ago; their surreal isolation is a product of human activity as much as natural selection.

## The Tsingy — Razor Limestone

The Tsingy de Bemaraha Strict Nature Reserve in western Madagascar is a UNESCO World Heritage Site of a kind found nowhere else on the same scale: a massif of limestone that groundwater has eroded over millions of years into a forest of razor-sharp pinnacles reaching 30 to 50 metres in height. Walking routes through the Tsingy — on suspended bridges and iron ladders between the pinnacles — are one of the world's most unusual hiking experiences, simultaneously geological spectacle and wildlife encounter (several lemur species have evolved specialised feet for navigating the sharp rock).

## Exceptional Lodges in a Challenging Country

Madagascar's accommodation scene has developed impressive eco-lodges and jungle camps despite the country's logistical challenges. [Jungle lodges](/categories/jungle-lodges) adjacent to national parks in the eastern rainforest combine guided lemur and chameleon tracking, birdwatching with expert naturalists, and genuine community benefit arrangements. The most compelling properties are those that function as conservation outposts — directly funding anti-poaching and habitat restoration around the lodge's concession area. Given Madagascar's extraordinary deforestation rate (approximately 40% of original forest cover lost), staying with operators that fund conservation is not simply ethical preference but practical necessity for the future of the species you have come to see.

## Practical Realities

Madagascar is one of the world's least-developed tourism infrastructures in a destination of this quality. Road conditions outside the capital are frequently poor — the Route Nationale 7 from Antananarivo to Tuléar (the country's main tourist road) takes three days to drive carefully. Domestic flights (Madagascar Airlines and private charters) are often more practical than road travel for covering the distances between the north and south. A reputable local operator is worth every cent for the logistical support alone — Madagascar rewards, but it rewards those who have planned carefully and embraced its difficulties as part of the experience.
