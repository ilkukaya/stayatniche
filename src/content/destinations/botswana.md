Botswana's approach to tourism is one of the most principled in Africa, and one of the most successful. When the country gained independence in 1966, it was among the poorest nations on earth, with 12 kilometres of paved road and an economy based almost entirely on beef exports. The discovery of diamonds transformed the national income; the decision to adopt a high-value, low-volume tourism model transformed the wilderness. Today, Botswana's wildlife populations are among the healthiest in Africa, its private concession system is the model other countries attempt to replicate, and the [safari lodges](/categories/safari-lodges) operating within the Okavango and Linyanti ecosystems represent the pinnacle of the African safari experience.


The Okavango is a geographical anomaly of striking beauty: a river that flows north from the Angolan highlands, spreads into a vast fan-shaped delta in the Kalahari Desert, and then evaporates: never reaching the sea. The result is 15,000 square kilometres of channels, islands, lagoons, and floodplains in the middle of one of the world's great desert systems. Permanent water in the Kalahari supports an ecosystem of extraordinary richness: elephant, buffalo, hippo, crocodile, leopard, lion, wild dog, and 540 species of birds.

The mokoro, a traditional dugout canoe propelled by a pole, is the Okavango's signature transport. Guided by a poler standing at the stern, you glide silently through channels fringed with papyrus and water lilies, past hippo pods and nesting egrets, to small raised islands where you continue on foot. Walking in the Okavango, following an experienced tracker through floodplain grass to where lion tracks lead toward a thicket, offers a quality of wildlife encounter that vehicle-based safaris cannot replicate.


Chobe National Park, in northern Botswana along the Chobe River, supports the highest concentration of African elephants anywhere on earth: an estimated 50,000 animals within the park, with herds of hundreds gathering at the river in the dry season to drink. The late afternoon boat cruise on the Chobe River, with elephants swimming between islands and hippos surfacing beside the boat, is among Africa's most reliably spectacular wildlife experiences.


The private concession system that distinguishes Botswana from most other safari destinations grants individual lodge operators exclusive use of large areas of wilderness, typically 100,000 to 300,000 hectares, in exchange for conservation fees paid to the government. Within their concession, operators can conduct night drives, off-road tracking, and walking safaris that would be impossible in national parks. Guest numbers in each area are strictly limited. The result is a level of exclusivity and intimacy, tracking a pack of wild dogs on foot, watching a leopard feed in a tree 20 metres from the vehicle, that defines Botswana as Africa's benchmark safari destination.

The camps themselves have evolved into something beyond accommodation. The finest Okavango lodges, elevated on raised platforms over floodplains, with canvas sides that can be rolled open to admit the night sounds and river air, combine genuine wilderness immersion with a level of design and culinary sophistication that would be remarkable in an urban hotel. Solar power, waste management, and community employment programmes are standard at the better properties.


Beyond the Okavango, Botswana's other wildlife landscapes offer contrasting experiences. The Makgadikgadi Pans, ancient salt lakes the size of Switzerland, shimmering white in the dry season sun, harbour habituated meerkat groups that researchers have studied for years. Visitors can join them at dawn, sitting at ground level while the meerkats use shoulders and heads as sun-warming platforms. In the wet season (December to March), the pans flood and host one of Africa's largest zebra migrations.

The Central Kalahari Game Reserve, one of the world's largest protected areas, offers a desert safari of an entirely different character: red sand dunes, sparse vegetation, and wildlife adapted to extreme aridity: brown hyena, cheetah, black-maned Kalahari lions, and the sociable weaver birds whose enormous communal nests weigh down acacia trees along the fossil river beds.
