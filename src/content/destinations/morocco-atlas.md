Morocco has been drawing travellers across the Strait of Gibraltar for millennia, and it earns every journey. The country occupies a remarkable geographic and cultural position: Mediterranean coast to the north, Atlantic to the west, Sahara to the south, and the High Atlas, a mountain range reaching nearly 4,200 metres, running diagonally through the middle. Each zone has its own distinct character, climate, and culture. Together they make Morocco one of the world's most diverse single-country journeys.


Morocco's four imperial cities, Fez, Marrakech, Meknes, and Rabat, each served as the country's capital at different periods of dynastic rule, and each retains a distinct personality. Fez is the most ancient and most disorienting: its medina, Fez el-Bali, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site that functions today essentially as it did in the 14th century. The tanneries where leather is still processed using medieval methods, saffron, poppy, indigo, and pigeon dung in round stone vats, are the city's most photographed sight, but the medina rewards those who spend days rather than hours within it.

Marrakech operates at a higher velocity: the Djemaa el-Fna square at dusk, with its snake charmers, storytellers, and mobile food stalls, is one of the world's great urban spectacles. The riads hidden behind the medina's blank exterior walls offer retreat: private courtyards with central fountains, orange trees, and the silence of thick earthen walls.


Morocco's riad tradition, the courtyard house turned boutique hotel, represents one of the world's great accommodation formats. The finest riads in Fez and Marrakech are works of craft as much as hospitality: hand-carved plasterwork (zellij tilework, muqarnas ceilings), cedar-wood latticework (mashrabiya screens), hand-painted ceilings in geometric patterns. The concept of the inward-looking house, blank exterior, elaborate interior, generates a particular quality of privacy and calm that feels increasingly rare and valuable.


The Erg Chebbi dunes near Merzouga in the southeast represent Morocco's most dramatic landscape. The dunes rise to 150 metres and extend for 22 kilometres, a sea of sculpted orange sand against a sky of extraordinary clarity. The standard experience involves a camel trek to a luxury desert camp at dusk, sleeping in Berber-style tents with proper beds and private bathrooms, watching the stars appear over the dune crest, and waking before dawn for the sunrise light on the dunes. It is theatrical, it is deliberate, and it is genuinely beautiful.


The High Atlas offers a completely different Morocco. Villages cling to valley walls above terraced fields of barley and almonds; Berber women in brightly coloured dress tend livestock on the high pastures; mule trains are the primary transport on tracks above the road line. The Toubkal Circuit (two to three days) ascends North Africa's highest peak, an achievable mountain trek that rewards with panoramas from the summit over a sea of lower ranges extending toward the Sahara. [Cave hotels](/categories/cave-hotels) and traditional kasbah lodges built from pisé (rammed earth) in the Draa Valley and Skoura palm groves offer some of the most authentic accommodation in the country.


Morocco is easily entered via Marrakech or Casablanca airports, with direct flights from London, Paris, Madrid, and major European hubs. Internal travel by CTM bus (comfortable, reliable, inexpensive) or rental car covers most of the country. The train network connects the northern cities efficiently. In the south, having a driver and 4WD for the Draa Valley and Sahara routes is both more convenient and more atmospheric than self-driving.
