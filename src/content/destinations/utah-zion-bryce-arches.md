Utah's canyon country is not scenery, it is geology at a scale that makes scenery seem insufficient as a concept. Stand at the rim of Canyonlands and you are looking at 300 million years of sedimentary record, layer upon layer of ancient seabeds, river deltas, and sand dunes compressed, lifted, and carved into a landscape that extends to every horizon. The five national parks here are not stops on a checklist; they are each a distinct and fully realized world.


The gateway communities around Utah's national parks have developed some of the most innovative outdoor accommodation in America. Zion Glamping Adventures places canvas tents within walking distance of Zion's east entrance with views over the White Cliffs. Moab has become a hub for luxury glamping operations that use the desert landscape as their amenity: fire pits under canopies of stars, outdoor soaking tubs, and the silence of the Colorado Plateau at night. Entrada at Snow Canyon offers casita-style rooms embedded in lava rock formations near St. George. And Bryce Canyon City, while modest, provides access to the park's hoodoos at dawn when the light is pink and gold and the tour buses have not yet arrived.


Zion is the most visited of Utah's parks and the most immediately dramatic. The Virgin River has carved a canyon 2,000 feet deep and in places barely 20 feet wide: The Narrows, a hike through the river itself, is among the most extraordinary trail experiences in the American West. The canyon walls are Navajo sandstone in shades of cream, orange, and deep red, striped with dark desert varnish. Angels Landing, a cable-assisted scramble to a summit with 1,000-foot drops on three sides, is as close to technical climbing as a maintained trail gets.


Bryce is not a canyon in the technical sense, it is a series of natural amphitheaters carved into the edge of the Paunsaugunt Plateau. The hoodoos, formed by cycles of freezing and thawing that shatter the limestone into spires, come in colors that shift from ivory to orange to deep vermillion with the quality of light. The Rim Trail follows the amphitheater edge for 11 miles; the Queen's Garden and Navajo Loop combination descends into the formations themselves. In winter, snow collects in the hollows between hoodoos and the scene becomes something from a different planet entirely.


Arches National Park contains over 2,000 natural stone arches, more than anywhere else on earth, and the 36-mile scenic drive passes dozens of them, from the enormous Landscape Arch (290 feet span) to the perfectly proportioned Delicate Arch standing alone on a slickrock bowl. Canyonlands, next door and far less crowded, divides into four distinct districts; the Island in the Sky district provides the most accessible views of a canyon system so vast it is difficult to fully register.


Utah's canyon country sits within one of the darkest sky regions in the continental United States. Canyonlands National Park is a certified Dark Sky Park; Capitol Reef and Natural Bridges National Monument (which holds an International Dark Sky Park designation) are within range for serious astrophotography. The silence of the desert at night, combined with a Milky Way overhead dense enough to cast shadows, is one of the most powerful experiences Utah offers, and many of the area's best accommodation properties have oriented their design specifically around the night sky.
