The Yoopers — Upper Peninsula residents — have a saying: the UP is God's country, and the rest of Michigan is just scenery. This is regional pride, but it is not entirely inaccurate. The Upper Peninsula contains old-growth forest that was never logged, 150 named waterfalls, three designated wilderness areas, and the most pristine freshwater coastline in North America on the southern shores of Lake Superior. Its very inconvenience — you have to want to get here, and getting here is a commitment — ensures that the people who come are the ones who understand what they're coming for.

## Why the Upper Peninsula Is Perfect for Niche Travel

The UP's accommodation culture is rooted in the hunting and fishing camp tradition that has operated here for over a century, but a new generation of operators has been transforming that tradition without abandoning its essential character. Tucked into the Hiawatha National Forest, small lakeside cabin clusters let you swim to your dock, kayak to dinner, and watch loons from the porch. Near Pictured Rocks, converted historic buildings in Munising offer proximity to one of America's most dramatic national lakeshore destinations. The western UP's Porcupine Mountains Wilderness State Park maintains rustic log cabins directly in the old-growth forest — some accessible only on foot or by ski — that represent one of Michigan's best-kept accommodation secrets.

## Pictured Rocks

Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore stretches for 42 miles along Lake Superior's south shore, with the defining feature being 15 miles of multicolored sandstone cliffs rising 50–200 feet directly from the lake. The colors — mineral streaks of iron oxide, manganese, copper, and limonite — create abstract patterns on the cliff faces that shift with weather and season. The cliffs can be seen by boat tour (the most popular approach), by kayak (the most intimate), or on foot along the North Country Trail that runs their length. Chapel Falls and Chapel Rock are accessible by trail from the Chapel Loop trailhead.

## The Porcupine Mountains

The Porcupines — "the Porkies" to everyone in the UP — contain the largest contiguous tract of old-growth northern hardwood and hemlock forest remaining in the Midwest. Lake of the Clouds, a glacial lake cupped in the mountains with the forest stretching to every horizon, is one of Michigan's finest views. The park's 90 miles of hiking trails range from accessible lakeshore paths to multi-day wilderness routes. In winter, the Porkies operate a small but excellent downhill ski hill and 26 kilometers of groomed cross-country trails through old-growth forest — a combination available nowhere else in Michigan.

## Tahquamenon Falls

Tahquamenon Falls State Park, in the eastern UP, contains what is arguably Michigan's finest waterfall scenery. The Upper Falls drops 50 feet across a 200-foot-wide face colored amber by the tannins of the surrounding peat bogs — in spring snowmelt, the flow rate is exceeded east of the Mississippi only by Niagara. The Lower Falls, two miles downstream, is a cluster of smaller falls around an island that you can reach by renting a rowboat — one of Michigan's most consistently delightful small experiences.

## Winter and the Snow Culture

The western UP near Ironwood and Bessemer typically receives more snow than anywhere else in the contiguous United States east of the Cascades. Blackjack Ski Resort and Indianhead Mountain offer modest but snow-certain downhill skiing. The Porcupine Mountains, Copper Country, and the Keweenaw Peninsula attract snowshoers and backcountry skiers who measure access to snow by the foot rather than the inch. The Keweenaw Peninsula — once the world's copper-mining capital — is a UNESCO Global Geopark with extraordinary industrial history layered beneath the snowpack.
