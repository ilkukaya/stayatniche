The Badlands force a recalibration of time. The formations you're looking at began eroding about 500,000 years ago and continue to erode at a rate of about one inch per year, which means the Badlands as they appear today will be completely gone in perhaps another 500,000 years. The fossils embedded in the formations are older still: the Oligocene-era beds here contain some of the richest prehistoric mammal deposits on Earth, including the ancestors of horses, rhinoceroses, and camels that roamed these prairies 30–35 million years ago.


The Badlands region has developed accommodation options that take genuine advantage of the landscape's scale and solitude. The Cedar Pass Lodge within Badlands National Park, operated by the Oglala Sioux Tribe, has been renovated with eco-cabins that deliver panoramic formation views from private porches at dawn and dusk, the optimal hours when the low-angle light turns the banded stratigraphy into something almost impossible to believe.

Outside the park, working ranches throughout the surrounding prairie offer [ranch stays and western heritage accommodations](/categories/ranch-stays) that connect visitors with the actual working landscape of the Great Plains. Several Lakota-owned properties around Pine Ridge offer cultural stays with guided trips to significant sites, giving context to the landscape that the national park interpretation alone cannot provide.


The Badlands formation area is divided into two sections. The North Unit, accessible from the Badlands Loop Road, contains the most dramatic pinnacle and butte formations and is the primary visitor area. The South Unit lies within the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation and requires coordination with the Oglala Sioux Tribe for access: this portion of the Badlands is less visited and, in many ways, more impressive for its solitude.

The hiking within the park rewards those willing to leave the road. The Notch Trail leads to a canyon viewpoint that feels genuinely remote despite being close to the visitor center. The Castle Trail traverses a long ridge with views across multiple formation zones. The grassland sections between formations harbor prairie dogs, pronghorn, and, in the park's restoration program, black-footed ferrets, one of the most endangered mammals in North America.


Sixty miles west of the Badlands, the Black Hills rise dramatically from the plains, a 125-mile-long range of pine-covered granite domes that the Lakota people have considered sacred for centuries and that the United States government took in violation of the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty following Custer's gold discovery in 1874. Today the Black Hills contain Custer State Park (one of America's finest state parks with a massive bison herd), Wind Cave National Park, Jewel Cave National Monument, and Mount Rushmore, along with the ongoing carving of Crazy Horse Memorial, which when completed will be the largest sculpture in the world.

Custer State Park's wildlife loop delivers encounters with bison herds, burro colonies (the "begging burros" that approach cars seeking handouts), prairie dogs, and pronghorn on a scale that rivals safari destinations. The State Game Lodge within the park, where Calvin Coolidge spent his 1927 summer "vacation", offers historic lodge rooms and cabin accommodations within one of the most wildlife-rich landscapes in the continental United States.


The Sturgis Motorcycle Rally, held annually in early August, brings approximately 500,000 motorcyclists to the Black Hills area for ten days, dramatically affecting accommodation availability and pricing throughout the region. Travelers not attending the rally should plan their Badlands and Black Hills visits carefully around this event.


Rapid City serves as the regional hub with a significant regional airport (RAP) connected to major hubs. The Badlands National Park entrance is about 75 miles east of Rapid City via Interstate 90. A vehicle is essential, public transportation does not serve the region. Entry fee: $30 per vehicle (valid 7 days).
