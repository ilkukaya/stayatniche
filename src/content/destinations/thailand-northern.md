Northern Thailand exists in a different register from the kingdom's sun-and-beach south. The landscape here is folded mountains, misty valleys, and teak forests stretching toward the borders of Myanmar and Laos. The culture draws from the ancient Lanna kingdom, a distinct civilization that predates the unification of Thailand and left behind a legacy of distinctive temple architecture, cuisine, and craft traditions that remain vigorously alive in Chiang Mai's old city and the villages that ring it.


Chiang Mai's old city, enclosed within a square moat, contains over 300 Buddhist temples built over seven centuries of Lanna rule. The finest, Wat Chedi Luang, Wat Phra Singh, Wat Chiang Man, are working religious sites where monks conduct morning prayers in the pre-dawn silence. The Sunday Walking Street and Saturday Market are among Southeast Asia's best craft markets, selling textiles, ceramics, and silverwork made in the surrounding villages.

The city's culinary scene deserves extended investigation. Northern Thai cuisine is markedly different from the dishes familiar from Thai restaurants abroad: khao soi (the rich coconut-curry noodle soup that defines Chiang Mai) is the essential order, accompanied by sai oua (herbed pork sausage), nam prik noom (green chili dip), and gaeng hang lay (a slow-cooked Burmese-influenced pork curry).


The mountains ringing Chiang Mai, particularly the Doi Inthanon and Doi Suthep-Pui national parks, offer trekking through ecosystems that range from tropical lowland jungle to temperate cloud forest at the summits. Doi Inthanon, at 2,565 metres, is Thailand's highest point, and the twin royal chedis near the summit are surrounded by remarkable mountain gardens that burst into colour during the cool season.

The [jungle lodges](/categories/jungle-lodges) found in the hills around Chiang Dao and Mae Taeng offer hands-on experiences among traditional villages, river valleys, and working rice farms. Several properties here are built entirely from local bamboo and timber, operate community benefit schemes with neighbouring villages, and offer guided treks to hill tribe settlements with genuine cultural exchange.


The area around Chiang Mai is home to a high concentration of elephant sanctuaries, a response to the collapse of the logging industry that once employed working elephants. The best sanctuaries, distinguished by their refusal to offer riding, performances, or chains, allow visitors to walk alongside elephants in the forest, assist with feeding, and help with bathing in the river. An afternoon spent this way, mud-streaked and in the company of a genuinely recovered animal, is one of Southeast Asia's most affecting travel experiences.


At the far north, where Thailand, Myanmar, and Laos meet at the Mekong River, the Golden Triangle offers a different kind of drama. The remote Anantara Golden Triangle resort here operates elephant conservation programmes and sits on a ridge with panoramic views over three countries simultaneously, a genuinely unusual perspective on mainland Southeast Asia's geography.


Chiang Mai is well connected from Bangkok by budget airline (one-hour flight), train (overnight sleeper), and bus. The city is compact and easily navigated by songthaew (shared red pickup truck taxi) or rented scooter. For the hill country beyond, hiring a driver or joining a guided trek is advisable, mountain roads can be challenging in a standard rental car.
