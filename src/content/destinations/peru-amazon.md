Peru is one of those countries that makes travel writers run out of superlatives. The concentration of extraordinary experiences, Inca architectural genius, Spanish colonial grandeur, Andean highland culture, Pacific coastal cuisine, and western Amazon rainforest, within a single country is staggering. Peru is also a country of genuine complexity: its history stretching from Chavin to Tiwanaku to Inca to Viceroyalty is one of the hemisphere's richest, and the 16th-century collision between Spanish conquistadors and the most sophisticated civilization in the Americas left an architectural and cultural legacy that rewards deep exploration.

## Machu Picchu: Still Essential

No amount of Instagram exposure diminishes the physical experience of Machu Picchu. The Inca citadel, built around 1450 and abandoned roughly a century later (probably following the Spanish conquest of Cusco), occupies a narrow ridge at 2,430 metres in the tropical Andes, surrounded on three sides by sheer drop to the Urubamba River gorge and on the fourth by the summit of Huayna Picchu mountain. The engineering required to build it, precision-fitted stone terraces, agricultural platforms, and ceremonial structures without mortar, using only stone tools and human labour, remains extraordinary.

The cloud forest setting adds a layer of mysticism that the highland ruins lack: mist rolls in from the Amazon basin in the afternoon, and the site frequently disappears from view, only to reappear dramatically as the clouds shift. Early morning entry (gates open at 6am) and timing your visit for the drier months rewards with the clearest sightlines and the fewest fellow visitors.

## Cusco: The Living Museum

Cusco was the capital of the Inca empire at its greatest extent, an empire that stretched from southern Colombia to central Chile and represented the largest political organisation in the pre-Columbian Americas. The Spanish demolished Inca temples and palaces but found the foundation stones too precisely fitted to remove, so they built their colonial churches and mansions directly on top. The result is a city where Inca masonry of extraordinary precision forms the ground floors of baroque churches, a palimpsest of civilisations visible in every block of the old city.

The Sacred Valley between Cusco and Machu Picchu, a lower, warmer, more oxygenated environment, offers some of the finest accommodation in the Andes. Converted haciendas in the valley with mountain views, traditional agricultural terraces still in use, and Quechua-speaking communities where textile weaving traditions have continued uninterrupted for 500 years provide context that Cusco's more commercial environment cannot offer.

## The Peruvian Amazon

The Madre de Dios river system in southeastern Peru protects one of the most biodiverse patches of rainforest on earth. Manu National Park and the adjacent Tambopata Research Reserve together safeguard a region where a single hectare can contain more tree species than the entire United Kingdom, and where clay licks attract hundreds of macaws in morning feeding frenzies that are among the most spectacular wildlife displays in the Americas.

The [jungle lodges](/categories/jungle-lodges) operating in this region represent the gold standard for Amazon accommodation: elevated platforms above the forest floor, guided walks with naturalist expertise that can identify any bird call or track any mammal footprint, and canopy towers that put guests at the level of the forest roof: a parallel world of monkeys, toucans, and harpy eagles invisible from below. The best lodges in Manu and Tambopata involve boats along the river and nights in deep jungle camp, providing access to areas where jaguar, giant river otter, and tapir are regularly encountered.

## Lake Titicaca and the Altiplano

At 3,812 metres above sea level, Lake Titicaca is the world's highest navigable body of water, a vast blue inland sea on the plateau between the Peruvian and Bolivian Andes. The Uros people live on floating islands constructed entirely from totora reed, the same material used to build their boats, their houses, and their cooking fires. A stay on a community-run reed island, sleeping in a totora house and waking to sunrise over the lake, is one of South America's most distinctive accommodation experiences.
