Ranthambore is where India's tigers live without apology. The 1,334 square kilometres of dry deciduous forest, grassland, and ancient lake that constitute this reserve in eastern Rajasthan are home to one of the highest concentrations of Bengal tigers in the world, and it is here, not in the more celebrated Corbett or Kanha, but here, within the shadow of a twelfth-century Rajput fort, that India offers its most compelling case for the survival of the world's most charismatic predator.

To stay at Aman-i-Khás is to position yourself at the precise intersection of two of India's defining cultural narratives: the Mughal imperial tradition of beautiful refinement and the ancient Rajput relationship with the land and its animals. The camp's ten tented pavilions draw consciously on both, with architecture that echoes the great canvas encampments of the Mughal emperors on campaign, soaring ceilings, bold geometric textiles, handcrafted carved furniture, while its location, pressed against the boundary of the tiger reserve, places it squarely within one of the subcontinent's most vital conservation stories.

The pavilions themselves are remarkable structures: each one a high-ceilinged canvas and wood room of genuine spaciousness, with proper hardwood floors, a deep freestanding soaking tub, and a verandah from which the early morning landscape of the reserve, misty, green, populated by sambar deer and the ever-present calls of peacocks, can be absorbed in contemplative quiet before the jeep departs for the morning safari.

Safaris at Ranthambore operate in six designated zones, and the quality of the Aman naturalists elevates the experience considerably above the standard park vehicle and government guide. These guides know individual tigers by name and territory, understand their seasonal movements, and read the alarm calls of langur monkeys and spotted deer with an expertise that translates directly into sighting success. On a good morning, and the statistics at Ranthambore are genuinely encouraging, a tigress will be sighted hunting, resting, or moving her cubs through the dry riverbed scrub with the unhurried authority of an animal that has never needed to fear anything in its landscape.

Evenings at Aman-i-Khás are their own reward. Around the fire pit, after dinner prepared with Rajasthani spices and the produce of the local market gardens, the naturalists review the day's sightings and chart plans for tomorrow. The stars above Rajasthan are exceptional, the air scented with dry grass and woodsmoke, and the distant call of a spotted deer, telling the forest that a predator is moving, is a sound that stays with you long after you've returned to the city.
