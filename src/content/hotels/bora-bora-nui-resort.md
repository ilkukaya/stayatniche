The overwater bungalow was invented in French Polynesia in the 1960s, and Bora Bora remains its spiritual home. Of all the islands in the Society group — Tahiti, Moorea, Huahine, Raiatea — Bora Bora presents the definitive version of the South Pacific dream: a volcanic peak (Mount Otemanu, 727 metres of basalt rising from the centre of the island) ringed by a barrier reef enclosing a lagoon of such extraordinary colour and clarity that it has become one of the most photographed bodies of water on Earth. The turquoise shades here defy easy comparison with anything in the Indo-Pacific; they are, quite simply, among the most beautiful that tropical water anywhere achieves.

Bora Bora Nui Resort occupies the most commanding position available on the island: a site on the outer reef with unobstructed views across the lagoon to Mount Otemanu, whose fluted basalt walls change colour through the day from blue-grey in the morning haze to deep green in the afternoon sun to the extraordinary copper-orange of the volcanic rock in the last hour of daylight. The overwater bungalows are positioned on long walkways over the lagoon, each one oriented to maximise this view.

The bungalows themselves are spacious by any standard — this is the "nui" (big) approach to overwater accommodation, with living areas generous enough for genuine habitation rather than the compressed luxury of some competitors. Thatch roofs maintain natural ventilation, glass floor panels reveal the reef fish below, and the deck steps descend directly to a private stretch of lagoon where snorkelling conditions are reliably excellent: black-tipped reef sharks patrol the shallows with complete indifference to swimmers, stingrays glide beneath the bungalows, and the reef fish population — butterfly fish, parrot fish, triggerfish, angelfish — is dense and colourful.

The social life of the resort concentrates at the main pool and the beach bar, where cocktails made with local vanilla and Tahitian citrus are served as the sun descends behind the outer reef. The spa draws on traditional Polynesian massage techniques — notably the hot stone lomi lomi massage and the Tifaifai ceremony — and the lagoon excursions include a shark and ray snorkelling safari that is one of Bora Bora's most iconic activities: wading among dozens of nurse sharks and stingrays in waist-deep water that feels simultaneously wild and perfectly safe.
