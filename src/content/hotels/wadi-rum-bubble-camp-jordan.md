Wadi Rum is one of those places where the word "landscape" feels entirely inadequate. The Valley of the Moon — as the ancient Nabataeans named it — spreads across 720 square kilometres of southern Jordan in a panorama of rose-red sandstone massifs, sweeping dune fields, and silence so complete it registers as a physical sensation. T. E. Lawrence wrote about it with barely concealed reverence, and astronauts have compared its otherworldly terrain to the surface of Mars. Sleeping here in a transparent bubble tent is, accordingly, an experience of extraordinary intensity.

The bubble camp occupies a plateau in the heart of the protected area, positioned to maximise both the surrounding geological theatre and the celestial display overhead. Wadi Rum's UNESCO World Heritage designation as a Protected Area has preserved its skies from development: on a clear night — and clear nights are the norm in this rain-shadow desert — the Milky Way is visible as a dense band of light from horizon to horizon, and shooting stars are commonplace. Conditions for stargazing here rival the world's premier dark sky reserves.

The bubbles themselves are generously proportioned, with air-conditioning that makes them habitable even during the heat of Jordanian summer afternoons, while the climate-control systems allow nights — which cool dramatically at altitude in the desert — to be kept perfectly comfortable. The transparent walls and ceiling ensure that from the king-sized bed, the view is essentially uninterrupted: cliff faces glowing in the moonlight, star fields of impossible density, and the occasional silhouette of a desert fox crossing the sand.

Evenings in camp follow the rhythm of Bedouin hospitality. Dinner is prepared in the traditional zarb style — meat and vegetables slow-cooked underground in a sealed pit — and served communally beneath the open sky, with sweet mint tea and conversation that stretches naturally into the night. The Bedouin guides who operate these camps carry generations of knowledge about the landscape, its wildlife, and its stars; their commentary on the night sky alone justifies the journey.

Days offer jeep safaris to Wadi Rum's most extraordinary formations — Lawrence's Spring, the Um Fruth rock bridge, Khazali Canyon with its Nabataean inscriptions — and camel rides to elevated viewpoints as the sun sets the sandstone ablaze. It is, taken in its entirety, one of the most complete and transporting desert experiences available anywhere on Earth.
