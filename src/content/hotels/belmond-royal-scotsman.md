The West Highland Line is, by the repeated judgement of the railway press and the travelling public, the most beautiful railway journey in the world. The line departs Glasgow and climbs through the Southern Highlands to Fort William, curves around the head of Loch Eil to Glenfinnan, where the famous 21-arch viaduct crosses the valley in a sweep of Victorian engineering grace made globally famous by the Harry Potter films, and continues north to Mallaig on the Sound of Sleat opposite the island of Skye. To cover this ground by ordinary InterCity service is a fine thing; to do it aboard the Royal Scotsman, moving at a deliberate pace that allows the landscape to be absorbed rather than consumed, is an experience of an entirely different order.

Belmond's Royal Scotsman operates a fleet of hand-crafted and carefully restored carriages of distinctive character: a warm palette of Highland tartan fabrics, polished wood panelling, brass fittings, and table lamps that cast a specifically Scottish warmth over the dining and lounge spaces. The train is small, carrying a maximum of 36 guests, and this scale is fundamental to the experience: the social world aboard is intimate in the way that the world of a country house party is intimate, with conversations developing naturally between people who have been thrown together by movement and shared purpose.

State Cabins are single or twin-bedded rooms with en-suite facilities on selected carriages, fitted with all the creature comforts necessary for multi-night journeys: thick blankets, reading lights positioned correctly, storage designed for the bags that travel brings. The Observation Car at the rear of the train extends to an open platform where guests stand in the Highland air as the landscape scrolls past: loch surfaces catching the afternoon light, red deer watching the train from hillsides, the white dots of black-faced sheep on the higher pastures.

Dining in the dining car is a formal but relaxed affair, as Scottish hospitality tends to be: white tablecloths and crystal in a room where the windows show, at breakfast, a Highland glen in morning mist, and at dinner, a loch going violet in the long northern dusk. The kitchen draws on the exceptional Scottish larder: venison, grouse, Highland lamb, lobster landed that morning from the west coast fisheries, and the salmon from rivers with names familiar from a thousand fishing stories. The whisky service, on a train that passes through or near some of the most distinguished distilleries in the world, is administered with the enthusiasm of people who believe in what they are offering.

Off-train excursions on the two-to-five day itineraries include distillery visits to Speyside or Islay, walks through Highland estates, castle visits, and boat trips on the lochs. The four-night Grand Tour covers the widest range of Highland landscape and experiences, and is the version that best represents the train's possibilities.

Book twelve months in advance for peak summer departures. The Royal Scotsman carries very few guests and its popularity far exceeds its capacity.
