The original Fram, Fridtjof Nansen's vessel, later used by Roald Amundsen on his Antarctic expedition, was the most important polar exploration ship of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. It went further north than any ship in recorded history, and further south than any wooden vessel before or since. It now rests in its own museum in Oslo, preserved as a national monument. Hurtigruten's MS Fram, the contemporary expedition ship that carries its name, operates in the spirit of that extraordinary legacy, taking expedition travellers into the same Arctic and Antarctic wildernesses with a level of commitment to genuine exploration that most cruise companies cannot match.

The MS Fram was designed specifically for polar conditions. Its hull is reinforced for ice navigation, its Zodiac fleet allows passengers to land on shores inaccessible to larger vessels, and its complement of sixteen specialist guides, marine biologists, glaciologists, ornithologists, historians, and photographers, provides an intellectual richness that transforms a voyage into something closer to a field school than a conventional cruise. Lectures in the ship's theatre alternate with Zodiac excursions to glacier faces, polar bear habitats, walrus colonies, and seabird cliffs teeming with thousands of little auks and puffins.

Svalbard, the Norwegian archipelago at 78 degrees north, is the ship's primary summer destination. This is polar bear country: the archipelago supports a population of roughly 3,000 bears, more than the human population of Longyearbyen, and encounters are a genuine probability rather than a tourist promise. Reindeer, Arctic fox, beluga whales, and the extraordinary summer spectacle of Atlantic walrus colonies on remote beaches are among the regular sightings that the ship's naturalists spend their expeditions seeking.

The experience of standing on the bow deck of the MS Fram as it moves through an ice field at midnight sun, when the light is golden and horizontal and the icebergs glow in colours that range from turquoise through white to the deepest blue, is one that travellers describe as permanently changing their relationship with scale, wilderness, and the natural world. This is not comfortable nature tourism; it is expedition travel with proper intellectual seriousness, proper physical engagement with wild environments, and the irreplaceable reward of genuine remoteness.

Cabins and suites range from practical explorer doubles to panoramic view suites, all designed for the practical realities of polar travel: good storage, warm materials, and windows large enough to make the most of the extraordinary light. The dining room serves Norwegian cuisine with an emphasis on Arctic ingredients: king crab, reindeer, Arctic char, and the cloudberry desserts that are among the most purely pleasurable things the Norwegian landscape produces.
