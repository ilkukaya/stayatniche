In 1952, the Italian Prime Minister Alcide De Gasperi stood in Matera and called it la vergogna d'Italia: the shame of Italy. The residents of the sassi, the ancient cave dwellings carved into the ravines of this Basilicata hilltown, were living without running water, electricity, or sanitation alongside their livestock. The government forcibly relocated them. The caves were abandoned.

Seventy years later, those same caves are among the most coveted hotel rooms in the world.

Sextantio Le Grotte della Civita is the work of architect and entrepreneur Daniele Kihlgren, whose philosophy of "conservative restoration", recovering what is there rather than imposing what is new, has transformed eighteen interconnected cave chambers into one of Italy's most singular hotels. The rooms retain their original walls of pale tuff stone, their vaulted ceilings, their rough-hewn floors. There are no painted walls, no decorative conceit that obscures the original material. What Kihlgren has added is placed with extraordinary discipline: quality bedding, discreet modern bathrooms, soft lighting. The result reads not as a hotel dressed as a cave, but as a cave that happens to be comfortable.

**The Rooms**

Each of the hotel's cave suites is different, because no two chambers in a 9,000-year-old inhabited hillside are alike. Some have low barrel-vaulted ceilings that press down with pleasing intimacy. Others open into tall, dramatic spaces where light falls from a ceiling aperture cut directly through the rock above. Terracotta and stone feature throughout. Beds are positioned against ancient walls. The bathrooms, integrated so thoughtfully that the modern fixtures read almost as archaeological insertions, are a genuine achievement of design restraint.

The temperature inside the caves stays naturally cool in summer, which in Matera's southern Italian heat is not a small thing.

**Matera Itself**

The hotel's greatest asset may be its address. Matera is one of the oldest continuously inhabited settlements on earth, and walking through the Sasso Caveoso after dinner, when the stone alleys are lit by oil lanterns and most of the day visitors have left, is to move through something that feels genuinely antique. Churches carved into cliff faces, ancient cisterns cut below the streets, stairways worn smooth by thousands of years of feet. The cathedral that presides over the ravine dates to the 13th century. The caves below it were in use long before Christ.

**Food and Wine**

The hotel restaurant serves traditional Lucanian cooking: the cuisine of Basilicata, a region still little known to most tourists but exceptional in its honest, ingredient-led cooking. Handmade pasta with wild boar ragù, salt cod with dried peppers, lamb slow-cooked with local herbs. The wine list focuses on southern Italian varietals: Aglianico del Vulture from the volcanic soils to the north, Primitivo from Puglia just across the regional border.

Sextantio Le Grotte della Civita is not a hotel that happens to be in a cave. It's a considered act of cultural recovery: a city's forgotten dwellings brought back to life as somewhere genuinely worth the journey to reach.
