Australia has always had a complicated relationship with its sky. The First Peoples of this continent mapped the night sky with extraordinary precision for tens of thousands of years, navigating by the Milky Way and reading weather from the movement of the Southern Cross. But for most visitors arriving from the Northern Hemisphere, the southern sky is a revelation, familiar constellations tilted at unfamiliar angles, new stars rising where none should be, and the Milky Way so dense and so vivid that it casts a faint shadow on clear nights.

BubbleTent Australia has built its entire proposition around the ambition of sharing that sky.

Situated in the rolling vineyard country of the Hunter Valley, Australia's oldest and most storied wine region, just two hours north of Sydney, the resort consists of a small number of carefully placed transparent bubble tents, each one angled to maximise the celestial view above the property's private land. The surrounding terrain helps: Hunter Valley has neither the light pollution of a major city nor the coastal haze that can sometimes trouble stargazers further east, and on still, moonless nights the view from inside a BubbleTent bubble is among the clearest available within a day's drive of any major Australian city.

The tent interiors are simple but well-considered: a large, comfortable bed oriented directly beneath the transparent ceiling, a small deck outside with a fire pit and a pair of generous camp chairs, and a thoughtful welcome hamper that typically includes a bottle from one of the valley's smaller producers. A telescope is provided for guests who want to push beyond naked-eye observation, along with a star map that helpfully identifies the major southern constellations, a genuine service for travellers unfamiliar with the inverted skyscapes of the south.

Days in the Hunter Valley unfold at their natural pace. The region contains more than 150 wineries within a relatively compact area, ranging from internationally recognised names to tiny boutique operations producing fewer than five hundred cases per year. A morning spent moving between cellar doors, tasting barrel samples and listening to winemakers explain their philosophy, gives way naturally to a long lunch on a veranda somewhere overlooking vines, and then, as the late-afternoon light turns the valley golden, the quiet journey back to your bubble to watch the sun descend behind the ranges.

At night, after the fire has burned low and the valley has fallen silent, you lie in your transparent sanctuary and watch the sky do what the southern sky does: perform, with quiet magnificence, for the very few people willing to find a dark enough place to watch.
