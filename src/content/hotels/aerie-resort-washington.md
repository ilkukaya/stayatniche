Lake Chelan occupies a singular place in the geography of Washington State: at 1,486 feet deep, the third deepest lake in North America, it plunges into the Cascade Mountains through a glacially carved trough of extraordinary length and narrowness, its dark blue water flanked by walls of basalt and scree that rise into peaks still carrying permanent snowfields in early summer. The lake is 50 miles long but in places barely a mile wide, and the effect is of navigating a Norwegian fjord that has somehow materialised in the American West.

The Aerie Resort exploits this extraordinary site with architectural confidence. Built into the clifftop above the lake's upper reaches, the property positions its guest rooms at the cliff's edge, some cantilevered over the rock face, others set into excavated terraces, with floor-to-ceiling glass walls that frame the lake and the opposing canyon walls in a view that changes dramatically with the light through the day. At dawn, the lake surface is still and mercury-pale; by afternoon it is an electric blue that matches the cloudless Washington sky; at dusk, the canyon walls catch the last horizontal light and glow amber while the water below darkens to near-black.

The Pacific Northwest has produced some of the continent's most assured contemporary architecture, and the resort's design reflects this regional confidence: rough-hewn local stone, weathered steel, and Douglas fir timber are combined with generous glazed surfaces and outdoor decks that make the most of the four seasons, spring wildflowers, summer heat on the water, autumn colour in the vine-covered slopes, and the stark beauty of the lake in winter when the surrounding mountains are fully snowbound.

The Chelan wine country, an American Viticultural Area in its own right, is one of Washington's most exciting wine producing regions, and the resort is an excellent base for exploring the dozen or more wineries that have established themselves on the lake's south-facing slopes. The remote village of Stehekin, accessible only by seaplane or ferry at the head of the lake, is a day trip of genuine character: no roads reach it from the outside world, and the community there has preserved something of the quality of life that existed in the Cascades before the twentieth century arrived.
