The Arabian oryx, the animal that almost certainly inspired the myth of the unicorn, viewed in profile with its long straight horns appearing as one, was declared extinct in the wild in 1972. Forty years of conservation effort, led in part by the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, has brought it back. Today, a protected population of more than 500 oryx roam the 225 square kilometres of protected desert that surrounds Al Maha Desert Resort & Spa, and on a good morning you can watch them from your private infinity pool while your coffee cools.

Al Maha, which means "Arabian oryx" in Arabic, has been built on a central premise: that the most luxurious experience in this desert is not the gold and marble of Dubai's city hotels, but the silence of the conservation reserve, the presence of animals that were once gone, and the texture of a landscape that existed before the city was built.

The forty-two suites are Bedouin in architectural spirit, low, sand-coloured structures with sweeping canvas overhangs that evoke the ma'jan encampments of nomadic Emirati life, but the interiors are unmistakably contemporary luxury: vast beds, handwoven textiles, deep freestanding baths, and floor-to-ceiling doors that open directly onto a private sun deck and that individual infinity pool, which appears to flow smoothly into the desert beyond. The wildlife viewing from this position is passive but profoundly affecting: oryx drift through the scrub, sand gazelles flash between the ghaf trees, and above it all, the falcons that the resort's falconer works with trace slow circles against the blue.

The falconry programme is a genuine high point, not a staged performance. Emirati falconry, which UNESCO recognises as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, has its roots in the survival economy of the desert, and Al Maha's falconers bring the same seriousness and reverence to the practice that defines it as a cultural institution. Guests learn to call the birds, to feel their weight on a gloved fist, to understand the specific intelligence of a species that has been partnered with humans in this landscape for more than four thousand years.

All-inclusive pricing removes the anxiety of the bill, and the dining, served either in the Bedouin-styled restaurant or under the stars on your private deck, is anchored in the flavours of the Arabian Gulf: slow-cooked lamb, saffron-scented rice, fresh-caught Gulf fish, and date confections that are the culinary heritage of a culture that has been perfecting them for centuries. In a city that often prioritises novelty over meaning, Al Maha offers something rarer: a place with roots.
