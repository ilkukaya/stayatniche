The Arabian oryx was declared extinct in the wild in 1972. Conservation effort, led in part by the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, has brought it back to a population of over 500 animals roaming the 225 square kilometres that surround Al Maha. You watch them from your private infinity pool in the morning, which is about as good as wildlife encounters get without leaving a luxury hotel.

Al Maha is the answer to a specific question: what does Dubai look like without the city? The answer is 225 square kilometres of protected desert where the silence is complete, the horizon is unbuilt, and the animals that once defined this landscape are back in it. The hotel's 42 suites echo the ma'jan tent camps of Bedouin tradition, low, sand-coloured structures with canvas overhangs, but the interiors are straightforwardly contemporary luxury. Vast beds, handwoven textiles, freestanding baths, and floor-to-ceiling doors that open onto a private deck and that infinity pool. The pool appears to flow into the desert. Sand gazelles flash between the ghaf trees beyond the fence. Falcons circle overhead.

The falconry programme is worth pausing over. Emirati falconry is a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, not a hotel activity bolted on for the brochure. Al Maha's falconers understand the practice as a cultural institution, and the sessions with guests reflect that seriousness. You learn to feel the weight of a falcon on a gloved fist, to call it, to understand something of how this partnership between human and raptor developed over four thousand years in this exact landscape.

All-inclusive means the bill is settled before arrival. Dining is either in the Bedouin-styled restaurant or on your private deck under the desert sky: Gulf fish, slow-cooked lamb, saffron rice, date preparations that the culture here has been refining for centuries. Adults only, 42 suites, properly remote from Dubai city while still being 45 kilometres from the airport. The combination is rarer than it sounds.
