There is no experience quite like the Gibbon Experience anywhere else on Earth. Forty metres above the floor of Laos's Bokeo Nature Reserve, on platforms of timber and cable suspended in the primary rainforest canopy, this extraordinary conservation project offers a treehouse experience that is simultaneously the most adventurous and the most ecologically meaningful available anywhere in Southeast Asia.

The project was established in 2002 by a conservation organisation working to protect the Bokeo Nature Reserve, a tract of largely intact primary rainforest in northwestern Laos that represents one of the last viable habitats of the black-crested gibbon (Nomascus concolor), one of the world's most endangered primates. The commercial model is elegant: ecotourist revenue directly funds the anti-poaching patrols that protect the reserve, and the presence of guests in the forest actively deters hunting. Every visitor is therefore, in a direct and measurable sense, contributing to the survival of one of Asia's rarest mammals.

The treehouses themselves are not luxury accommodation by any conventional measure: the platforms are reached by zipline, the sleeping quarters are basic (though comfortable and clean), the food is simple jungle cooking prepared by local staff on wood fires. What they offer instead of luxury is something more valuable: the experience of spending the night in the primary rainforest canopy, 40 metres above the forest floor, surrounded by the full sensory complexity of a functioning tropical ecosystem.

At dawn, the gibbons announce themselves with calls of startling power and beauty: long, haunting whoops that carry for kilometres through the forest and represent the territorial singing of family groups beginning their day. On most mornings during the dry season, guests can observe gibbon families swinging through the canopy within viewing distance of the platforms, moving with a speed and grace that seems to belong to a different physical category than anything on the ground. This is not a zoo or a rehabilitation centre; these are wild animals in their natural habitat, observed with minimal intrusion.

The zipline network connects the treehouses through the canopy in a series of soaring flights through primary forest. The longest ziplines cover several hundred metres, and the experience of flying through the canopy at 40 metres altitude, trees passing in a blur of green, the forest floor invisible below, is one of the most purely exhilarating activities available in Southeast Asian travel.
