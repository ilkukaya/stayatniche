The alarm goes off at 4 a.m. in Aguas Calientes and you dress in darkness, joining a small line of similarly sleep-deprived but quietly excited travellers at the bus stop below the ruins. The road up to Machu Picchu's entrance winds through cloud forest in a series of sharp switchbacks, and by the time the bus deposits you at the gate, the sky above the surrounding peaks is just beginning to grey.

The route to the Sun Gate — Inti Punku in Quechua — climbs away from the main citadel along a well-maintained Inca path that the conquistadors never found and that most modern tourists never take. Your guide sets a steady pace through humid cloud forest, pointing out orchids and the bromeliad-draped ruins of Inca waypoints along the path. After roughly ninety minutes of climbing, the trail rounds a final corner and the Sun Gate appears — and through it, for the first time, Machu Picchu.

This is the view. The citadel laid out in full below you, emerald terraces cascading down the mountain, the iconic peak of Huayna Picchu rising behind it, the Urubamba River looping far below in its green valley. If you've timed the approach correctly, the sun is still rising as you arrive, burning off the morning mist in real time and casting long golden shadows across the stone.

The descent to the citadel proper takes twenty minutes, and then the full day of exploration begins. Your guide navigates the complex with obvious expertise — the Temple of the Sun's trapezoidal windows aligned with the winter solstice sunrise, the Intihuatana stone's function as a ritual and astronomical anchor, the hydraulic engineering of the Inca water system still running cleanly after five centuries.

**Practical tips:** Book entrance tickets well in advance — daily visitor numbers are strictly capped. Altitude is approximately 2,430 metres; acclimatise for at least two days in Cusco (3,400m) beforehand.
