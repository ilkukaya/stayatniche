The camel's gait is stranger than you expect — a front-back rocking that takes fifteen minutes to absorb before something clicks and the rhythm becomes almost meditative. By the time Merzouga village has disappeared behind the first ridge of dunes and the only sound is wind on sand and the soft footfall of your camel train, any residual awkwardness has dissolved into something closer to contentment.

Erg Chebbi is one of Morocco's two great sand seas, a genuinely vast expanse of Saharan dunes that rise up to 150 metres above the surrounding flat hamada. The colours change constantly with the light — pale cream in the middle of the day, burning orange as the afternoon progresses, then red, rose, and finally a deep violet as the sun drops behind the Algerian border mountains to the west. Your Berber guide leads the caravan with the unhurried confidence of someone who grew up reading this landscape and knows precisely where the camp has been set for the night.

Desert camp is more comfortable than the word "camp" suggests. Berber-patterned rugs cover the ground inside the communal tent; lanterns cast warm light on the dinner laid out on low tables. The food is traditional Moroccan: tagine, couscous, flatbread with olive oil, and sweet mint tea poured from a theatrical height. After dinner, drums appear and the guides perform music that has a direct lineage to the trans-Saharan trade routes that made this region wealthy for centuries.

The night sky above the Sahara is categorical. Light pollution is nonexistent for hundreds of kilometres in every direction, and on a clear night the Milky Way is bright enough to navigate by, the satellite trails easy to distinguish from the static points of stars.

**Practical tips:** Desert temperatures drop sharply after dark, even in summer. Pack a warm layer regardless of the season. The return trek begins around 6 a.m. to catch the sunrise dune colours.
