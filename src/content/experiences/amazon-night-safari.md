The Amazon Rainforest does not go to sleep at night, it simply changes shifts. The species that were visible in daylight retreat to shade and high canopy, replaced by an entirely different cast of creatures that the daylight tourists never see. The night safari is how you access this parallel world, and it is frequently the experience Amazon visitors remember most vividly.

Departure from the dock near Manaus comes after sunset, when the river has settled to a mirror surface and the equatorial darkness is genuine and total away from the city's light dome. The motor canoe moves quietly along tributaries that branch from the main Negro River, its spotlight sweeping the vegetation on the banks. The first caimans usually appear within fifteen minutes, their eyes return a deep orange-red from the torchlight, visible from surprising distances. Your guide can read their size and species from the eye-shine and spacing alone.

The river section transitions to a guided walk into the surrounding forest, and here the density of life becomes extraordinary. The litter layer between tree roots pulses with leaf-cutter ants moving in perfect lines. Tree frogs the size of thumbnails cling to the underside of broad leaves, their skin colouring ranging from vivid green to the exact brown of bark. Your guide moves with quiet confidence, stopping abruptly to illuminate a coiled boa high in a riverbank tree, or to locate a large forest tarantula at the entrance to its silk-lined burrow: impressive if you are brave, terrifying if you are not.

Above, the canopy occasionally reveals a pair of large, luminous eyes: night monkeys (also called owl monkeys), South America's only nocturnal primates, watching the intrusion from the safety of a high branch with intelligent, patient calm.

**Practical tips:** Long sleeves, long trousers, and insect repellent are essential. Yellow fever vaccination is strongly recommended before any Amazon travel. Most guides speak English and Portuguese.
