Every year, as autumn turns to winter in the Swedish subarctic, an extraordinary process begins on the frozen Torne River near the village of Jukkasjärvi. Teams of ice harvesters move onto the ice and begin cutting, extracting blocks of crystal-clear river ice, some up to 90 centimetres thick, that will form the structural bones of a building that does not yet exist. International artists arrive with sketches and chisels. Architects translate blueprints into construction guidelines for a medium unlike any other. By December, where there was frozen tundra in October, there is a palace, sculpted, lit, and open for guests to sleep in at temperatures well below freezing.

Ice hotels are arguably the most remarkable seasonal phenomenon in the entire world of hospitality.


The ICEHOTEL in Jukkasjärvi uses approximately 5,000 tonnes of ice and snow from the Torne River each year: harvested, transported, and assembled over a construction period that runs from October through December. Artists from more than 50 countries compete annually for the opportunity to design the art suites, submitting detailed proposals that are judged on artistic merit and structural feasibility. The winning designers then spend weeks carving their concepts into room-scale ice sculptures using chainsaws, angle grinders, chisels, and hand tools.

The structural technique is called snice, a compressed mixture of snow and ice that acts as both mortar and sculptural material. Walls are assembled from ice blocks bonded with snice and insulated with additional snow packing. The result maintains a natural temperature of -5°C to -8°C inside the sleeping rooms, stable regardless of exterior conditions, controlled by snow insulation rather than mechanical systems. The ice walls glow with an otherworldly blue-green luminescence when lit from within, a quality unique to this material and unlike anything achievable with any other building medium.


The experience of sleeping in an ice hotel room is genuinely distinct from any other accommodation. Upon checking in to an ice room, you receive an expedition-grade Arctic sleeping bag rated to -35°C, far below the room temperature you'll actually encounter, along with a thermal underlayer and wool socks. The hotel staff demonstrate the correct technique for using the sleeping system: remove outer clothing before entering the bag, place clothes inside the bag with you to warm them for morning, and resist the instinct to cover your face.

The room itself is strikingly beautiful. Ice walls carved into bas-reliefs or free-standing sculptures surround a sleeping platform (also ice, covered with reindeer hides) and reflect light in qualities impossible to replicate in any other material. The silence inside an ice room is absolute: snow absorbs sound completely, and the result is a quiet so total that your own breathing seems loud.

Many guests report sleeping better in an ice hotel room than expected. The cold air, paradoxically, promotes deep sleep, the body's natural sleep temperature is slightly below waking temperature, and the ice room environment supports this. Most guests wake refreshed and often describe the experience as one of the most distinctive and unexpectedly comfortable nights of their travelling lives.


Most ice hotels maintain both cold ice rooms and heated alternative accommodation. At ICEHOTEL Jukkasjärvi, warm options include heated log cabins and wooden chalets: comfortable, Scandi-designed, and positioned to give access to all the public ice spaces (the bar carved entirely from ice, the ceremonial hall with its ice chandeliers, the art suite gallery corridors) without requiring a night at sub-zero temperature. This combination is ideal for families and groups with mixed cold-tolerance.

The ice bar, a standard feature at most ice hotels globally, is open to day visitors as well as overnight guests. Drinking from a carved ice glass in an ice-walled room, where the ambient temperature is maintained below -5°C regardless of how many bodies are in the space, is an experience worth pursuing even without an overnight stay.


Every ice hotel has a season, and every season has an end. As spring temperatures rise, typically in April or May in northern Sweden, the ice begins to return. First the exterior surfaces show melt; then interior spaces show the first signs of drip. The hotel closes its doors, and over the following weeks the entire structure, every carved wall, every sculpted art suite, every ice bar shelf, melts back into the Torne River from which it came.

This ephemerality is central to the ice hotel philosophy. You cannot revisit the exact same property; this year's art suites are gone by June, and next year's designs have not yet been conceived. The urgency this creates is part of what makes ice hotel bookings feel meaningful, you are booking something that genuinely will not exist for long.


**Jukkasjärvi, Sweden** is the original and benchmark, with ICEHOTEL's 65+ suites, extraordinary artistic programme, and year-round operation (a permanent cold section maintained by refrigeration through summer). **Finland's SnowVillage** near Kittilä and the **Kemi SnowCastle** offer Finnish interpretations of the form. **Norway's Sorrisniva Igloo Hotel** near Alta combines ice sleeping with one of Norway's prime aurora borealis locations, Alta sits directly under the auroral oval. **Canada's Hôtel de Glace** in Quebec City brings the concept to North America with French-Canadian architectural character.


The season typically runs mid-December through late March. Book ice rooms six to nine months in advance for the most popular properties, warm rooms have more availability. Pack merino wool base layers and trust the hotel's sleeping equipment completely.

For complementary Arctic winter experiences, [bubble hotels](/categories/bubble-hotels) in Finnish Lapland offer warmer but equally spectacular northern lights viewing from transparent domes, making an ideal two-destination Arctic itinerary.
