Few accommodation experiences connect you with the deep history of human habitation like a cave hotel. These extraordinary properties occupy spaces that have sheltered people for thousands, in some cases tens of thousands, of years, carved by hand from volcanic tufa, sandstone, and limestone, and now transformed with extraordinary care into some of the most atmospheric rooms available anywhere in the world. Staying in a cave hotel is not simply booking a room with unusual walls. It is inhabiting a piece of geological and human history.


Cappadocia, Turkey's premier cave hotel destination, has been inhabited for over 2,000 years, though archaeological evidence suggests human presence in the region's cave systems stretching back considerably further. The region's unique volcanic tufa landscape, sculpted by centuries of erosion into the famous fairy chimneys and dramatic valleys, was ideally suited to cave dwelling: the tufa is soft enough to carve with basic tools, yet hardens on exposure to air, creating durable and thermally stable living spaces.

Matera, in southern Italy, takes the history even deeper. The Sassi di Matera, the ancient cave district carved into the ravines of Basilicata, has been continuously inhabited for approximately 9,000 years, making it one of the oldest human settlements on earth. After decades as a symbol of poverty (residents were forcibly relocated in the 1950s), the Sassi were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site and have since been transformed into some of Italy's most remarkable boutique hotels, their ancient forms now housing designer furniture and locally sourced restaurant menus.


The defining characteristic of cave accommodation is its relationship with temperature. Rock maintains a remarkably consistent thermal mass year-round, cave interiors typically hover between 15°C and 18°C regardless of exterior conditions. In summer, this natural cooling is a genuine luxury, a cave suite staying comfortable without any mechanical air conditioning when exterior temperatures in Cappadocia or southern Spain push past 35°C. In winter, the same thermal mass retains warmth efficiently, meaning heating requirements are substantially lower than in conventional construction.

This natural climate control is both practically advantageous and deeply pleasurable. There is something fundamentally comfortable about being surrounded by material that regulates its own temperature: the rock feels cool to the touch in summer, the air inside neither damp nor dry. The cave's relationship with its environment is ancient and well-calibrated.


Authentic cave hotel rooms are cut directly into natural rock formations: the walls, ceiling, and often the floor are living stone, shaped over geological time and refined by human hands. Within these ancient shells, modern cave hotels install heated floors, rainfall showers, fine linens, and selected art, creating a striking dialogue between ancient and contemporary.

The light quality in cave rooms is distinct. Natural light enters through carved doorways and window embrasures, falling at angles determined by the original rock face rather than an architect's plan, often creating effects of extraordinary beauty at certain times of day. Some guests note mild claustrophobia concerns before arrival, but the reality of a well-converted cave room is generally spacious rather than confining: the thickness of the rock walls creates a profound sense of shelter and security that most guests find deeply calming.


**Cappadocia, Turkey** is the unrivalled global capital of cave hotel accommodation. The towns of Goreme, Uchisar, and Urgup contain cave hotels ranging from boutique guesthouses to five-star properties with rooftop infinity pools overlooking the otherworldly landscape. Sunrise hot air balloon flights above the fairy chimneys are among travel's great spectacles: book separately from your accommodation, as demand is intense.

**Santorini, Greece** offers cliff-side cave suites carved into the volcanic caldera rim, with terraces hanging above one of the world's most spectacular sea views. Here the cave aesthetic combines with whitewashed Cycladic architecture for an unmistakably Greek result, arguably the most photogenic cave hotels on earth.

**Matera, Italy** provides an hard to beat historical experience: sleeping within the Sassi means inhabiting one of humanity's oldest continuously occupied dwellings. **Granada, Spain** offers cave houses, cuevas, in the hills above the Alhambra. **New Mexico, USA** has developed cave-adjacent adobe stays in the tradition of Ancestral Puebloan cliff dwellings. **Australia's** Coober Pedy, an underground opal-mining town in the outback, offers cave motel rooms that are genuinely unlike anything elsewhere.


The best cave hotels in Cappadocia and Santorini book heavily during spring (April–May) and autumn (September–October) when temperatures are ideal and visitor numbers peak. For Cappadocia, book at least two months ahead. Cave hotels reward guests who slow down, the atmosphere encourages lingering over breakfast, sitting with a glass of local wine as the sun sets over ancient valleys, and simply being present in a space that carries millennia of accumulated human story.

For complementary geological drama at a different scale, [cliffside hotels](/categories/cliffside-hotels) offer the outside-the-rock perspective that cave hotels provide from within.
